<NoteSection type="goals">

In this note, we establish the foundational concepts of differential calculus:

1. The formal definition of a limit
2. The derivative as a limit of difference quotients
3. Basic differentiation rules
4. The connection between derivatives and tangent lines

</NoteSection>

<NoteSection type="notation">

Throughout this note, we adopt the following conventions:

- $\mathbb{R}$ denotes the set of real numbers
- $f: A \to B$ indicates a function from set $A$ to set $B$
- $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ means the limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $a$ equals $L$
- $f'(x)$ or $\frac{df}{dx}$ denotes the derivative of $f$ with respect to $x$

</NoteSection>

## The Concept of a Limit

<NoteSection type="definition" title="Limit of a Function">

Let $f$ be a function defined on some open interval containing $a$, except possibly at $a$ itself. We say that the **limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $a$ is $L$**, written

$$
\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L
$$

if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that

$$
0 < |x - a| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
$$

</NoteSection>

<NoteSection type="remark">

The $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition captures the intuitive notion that $f(x)$ can be made arbitrarily close to $L$ by taking $x$ sufficiently close to (but not equal to) $a$. This formalization, due to Weierstrass, provides the rigorous foundation for all of calculus.

</NoteSection>

## The Derivative

<NoteSection type="definition" title="Derivative at a Point">

The **derivative** of a function $f$ at a point $a$, denoted $f'(a)$, is defined as

$$
f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}
$$

provided this limit exists. When it exists, we say $f$ is **differentiable** at $a$.

</NoteSection>

<NoteSection type="theorem" title="Power Rule">

For any real number $n$ and function $f(x) = x^n$, we have

$$
\frac{d}{dx}(x^n) = nx^{n-1}
$$

</NoteSection>

<NoteSection type="proof">

Using the definition of derivative:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}(x^n) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^n - x^n}{h}
$$

Expanding $(x+h)^n$ using the binomial theorem:

$$
(x+h)^n = x^n + nx^{n-1}h + \binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h^2 + \cdots + h^n
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{(x+h)^n - x^n}{h} = nx^{n-1} + \binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h + \cdots + h^{n-1}
$$

Taking the limit as $h \to 0$, all terms containing $h$ vanish, leaving $nx^{n-1}$.

</NoteSection>

## Differentiation Rules

<NoteSection type="theorem" title="Sum and Product Rules">

Let $f$ and $g$ be differentiable functions. Then:

**Sum Rule:**
$$
(f + g)'(x) = f'(x) + g'(x)
$$

**Product Rule:**
$$
(fg)'(x) = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x)
$$

</NoteSection>

<NoteSection type="example">

Consider $f(x) = x^3 + 2x^2 - 5x + 7$. By the sum rule and power rule:

$$
f'(x) = 3x^2 + 4x - 5
$$

For $g(x) = x^2 \sin(x)$, by the product rule:

$$
g'(x) = 2x \sin(x) + x^2 \cos(x)
$$

</NoteSection>

## The Chain Rule

<NoteSection type="theorem" title="Chain Rule">

If $g$ is differentiable at $x$ and $f$ is differentiable at $g(x)$, then the composite function $f \circ g$ is differentiable at $x$, and

$$
(f \circ g)'(x) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)
$$

In Leibniz notation, if $y = f(u)$ and $u = g(x)$, then

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}
$$

</NoteSection>

<NoteSection type="remark">

The chain rule is perhaps the most important differentiation technique, as it allows us to differentiate composite functions. It also provides the foundation for implicit differentiation and related rates problems.

</NoteSection>
