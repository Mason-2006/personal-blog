<NoteSection type="goals">

We introduce the notion of convergence for real sequences, establish key properties, and state the completeness axiom.

</NoteSection>

<NoteSection type="notation">

- $(a_n)_{n \geq 1}$ or $(a_n)$ denotes a sequence of real numbers.
- $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$ is the set of positive integers.

</NoteSection>

## Convergence

<NoteSection type="definition" title="Convergence of a Sequence">

A sequence $(a_n)$ of real numbers **converges** to a limit $L \in \mathbb{R}$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that

$$
n \geq N \implies |a_n - L| < \varepsilon
$$

We write $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L$, or simply $a_n \to L$.

</NoteSection>

<NoteSection type="example">

The sequence $a_n = 1/n$ converges to $0$. Given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $N > 1/\varepsilon$ (by the Archimedean property). Then for $n \geq N$:

$$
|a_n - 0| = \frac{1}{n} \leq \frac{1}{N} < \varepsilon
$$

</NoteSection>

<NoteSection type="theorem" title="Uniqueness of Limits">

If $a_n \to L$ and $a_n \to M$, then $L = M$.

</NoteSection>

<NoteSection type="proof">

Suppose $L \neq M$. Set $\varepsilon = |L - M|/2 > 0$. There exist $N_1, N_2$ such that $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$ for $n \geq N_1$ and $|a_n - M| < \varepsilon$ for $n \geq N_2$. For $n \geq \max(N_1, N_2)$:

$$
|L - M| \leq |L - a_n| + |a_n - M| < 2\varepsilon = |L - M|
$$

This is a contradiction, so $L = M$.

</NoteSection>

## Cauchy Sequences

<NoteSection type="definition" title="Cauchy Sequence">

A sequence $(a_n)$ is **Cauchy** if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that

$$
m, n \geq N \implies |a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon
$$

</NoteSection>

<NoteSection type="theorem" title="Completeness of the Reals">

A sequence of real numbers converges if and only if it is Cauchy.

</NoteSection>

<NoteSection type="remark">

Completeness is what distinguishes $\mathbb{R}$ from $\mathbb{Q}$. The sequence of rational approximations to $\sqrt{2}$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb{Q}$ but does not converge in $\mathbb{Q}$. The real numbers are, by construction, the completion of the rationals.

</NoteSection>
